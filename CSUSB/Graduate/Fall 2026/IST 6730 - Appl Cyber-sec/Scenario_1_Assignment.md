### Suggested memo structure

1. Purpose/overview of the new technology
2. Key cybersecurity issues to consider
3. Recommendations / mitigation strategies
4. Conclusion

### Key issues affecting cybersecurity in technology adoption

**1. Risk assessment and due diligence**  
Before adoption, the organization should conduct a formal risk assessment — identifying what data/systems the new technology will touch, what new attack surface it introduces, and whether the risk is proportional to the benefit.

**2. Vendor and third-party risk**  
New technology often means a new vendor relationship. Talking points: vendor security posture, SLAs around breach notification, data handling practices, and the risk of supply chain attacks (e.g., SolarWinds is a commonly cited example).

**3. Integration with legacy systems**  
New tech rarely exists in isolation — it has to interface with existing infrastructure. Poorly secured integration points (APIs, data pipelines) are a common source of vulnerabilities.

**4. Data privacy and regulatory compliance**  
Depending on industry, new technology may trigger compliance obligations (GDPR, HIPAA, CCPA, PCI-DSS). A good angle: compliance isn't just legal risk, it directly shapes security requirements (encryption standards, data residency, retention policies).

**5. Identity and access management**  
Who gets access to the new system, and how is that access controlled/verified? Talking points: role-based access control, MFA requirements, and the principle of least privilege during rollout.

**6. Shadow IT and unsanctioned adoption**  
If IT/security is slow to formally introduce new technology, employees may adopt unauthorized tools on their own — a good point about why proactive, well-communicated rollouts reduce shadow IT risk.

**7. Employee training and change management**  
New technology changes user behavior, and untrained users are a major vulnerability. Cover both technical training (how to use it securely) and awareness training (phishing/social engineering risks tied to the new tool).

**8. Network security implications**  
Does the new technology require new network segments, open new ports, or introduce new endpoints (IoT devices, cloud services)? This ties back to firewall/segmentation concepts if you want continuity with prior coursework.

**9. Cloud vs. on-premise security considerations**  
If the technology is cloud-based, discuss the shared responsibility model — what the vendor secures vs. what the organization must secure — and data sovereignty concerns.

**10. Encryption and data protection**  
Address encryption both at rest and in transit, and whether the new technology meets the organization's existing data protection standards.

**11. Testing and phased rollout**  
A pilot/testing phase (sandboxing, staged deployment) lets security teams identify vulnerabilities before organization-wide exposure — a strong point for showing proactive risk management.

**12. Incident response and monitoring updates**  
New technology may require updates to the incident response plan, SIEM/logging configuration, and monitoring tools so the security team can actually detect issues involving the new system.

**13. Insider threat during transition periods**  
Transitions/rollouts are often periods of confusion where unusual access patterns are harder to detect — worth flagging as a temporary elevated-risk window.

**14. Business continuity and disaster recovery**  
Discuss how the new technology affects backup procedures, redundancy, and recovery time objectives (RTO/RPO) if it fails or is compromised.

**15. Budget and resource allocation for security controls**  
A practical business angle: security shouldn't be an afterthought bolted on after adoption — cost of proper security controls should be budgeted into the technology's total cost of ownership.

**16. Executive sponsorship and security culture**  
Successful, secure adoption typically requires leadership buy-in and clear governance/policy updates — a good closing point tying technical issues back to organizational strategy.

### Optional framing devices for structure

- **CIA Triad** (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) — organize your issues under these three pillars
- **NIST Cybersecurity Framework** (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) — useful if your course has covered it, gives a clean way to bucket 15+ points into 5 sections
- **People, Process, Technology** — a classic business framing that works well in memos
