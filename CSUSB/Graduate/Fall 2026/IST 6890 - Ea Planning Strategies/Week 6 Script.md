
## **Slide 13 - Visions**
"That wraps up Chapter 10. My name is Mario, and Up to this point we've been inside the IT department. Standards are written by architects, for architects, and most business leaders never see them.

Chapter 11 moves us to the business side. We're going from a technical artifact to a business one: Visions.

Before we go into it, I thought it would be helpful to understand why do Visions exist in the first place? In many organizations, business leaders set the strategy, but the IT department is left to guess what technology that strategy needs. IT might spend a year building a system that seems urgent, only to find out it isn't what the business cares about most. Or it might put money into an area that leadership was planning to shrink. Nobody did anything wrong. Business and IT just weren't looking at the same plan. Visions fix that by giving both sides one shared picture of where the organization is going, so every dollar spent on technology moves it in the right direction.

I'll explain how that works, and why it matters, over the next few slides. We'll cover what Visions are, how they're used, why they matter, and the specific types of Visions organizations use."


## **Slide 14 - What Visions are**
"So what exactly is a Vision? Put simply, it's a shared picture of where the business is headed, and business and IT agree on it together.

There are four things that make a Vision a Vision.

First, it's **business-focused**. A Vision talks about what the organization does, like serving customers, selling products, or running operations. It doesn't talk about servers or software. If you handed one to the CEO, they'd understand it without a translator.

Second, it's a **dual artifact**. That means two different groups use it: business executives and architects. It's the common ground where both sides meet.

Third, it looks **three to five years ahead**. It isn't about next week's project. It's about where the organization wants to be a few years from now.

And fourth, it's **simple by design**. Visions are usually one colorful page, because busy executives aren't going to read a 40-page technical document.

A good real-world example is a school district's technology plan. The school board and the superintendent might agree that within three years every student will have a laptop and parents will be able to check grades online. That's the Vision. It says where the district is going and why, and it's simple enough for the board to approve. It doesn't say which laptop brand to buy or how to set up the network. The IT team figures that out later."


## **Slide 15 - How Visions Guide IT Investment**
"So how does a Vision actually get made, and what happens to it afterward?

Visions come out of the **Strategic Planning process**. Architects are the ones who physically draw them up, but the ideas come from business executives. The architect's job is to listen, ask good questions, and turn what leadership wants into something clear enough for everyone to agree on.

Once a Vision is agreed on, it gets put to work in four steps.

First, it helps the organization **focus**. The money goes to the areas that matter most to the strategy.

Second, it helps **identify** candidate IT projects, the specific initiatives that would move things forward.

Third, it helps **prioritize** those projects. Some ideas come from the top, from executives. Others come from the bottom up, from a department manager with a specific need. The Vision gives everyone a fair way to decide what goes first.

And fourth, the chosen projects are **launched** into the Initiative Delivery process, which is where they actually get built.

If we go back to the school district example. The Vision says every student gets a laptop and parents can check grades online. That **focuses** spending on student technology. It **identifies** two projects: a laptop rollout and a parent portal. Now say a principal asks for new cameras for the front office. That request gets **prioritized** against the Vision, and it probably waits, because the laptops come first. Then the laptop rollout is **launched** as a real project with a budget and a team.

This is why Visions act as the bridge from strategy to real, executable IT work. And they don't sit on a shelf. They're permanent, and they get re-approved every year once the business strategy is renewed."


## **Slide 16 - Why Do Visions Matter?**
"Remember the problem from the start of this chapter: business and IT weren't looking at the same plan. This slide is the answer to that problem.

Visions matter because they tie IT spending directly to where the business wants to go long-term. They do that by answering four simple questions.

**How much** should we spend on IT? The Vision gives leaders a sense of how big the investment needs to be to reach their goals.

**Where** should the money go? It points spending at the areas that matter most, and away from the ones that don't.

**What** types of investments do we need? New systems, upgrades, or replacing something old?

And **when** should those investments happen? What comes first, and what can wait?

Going back to our school district: the Vision tells the board they'll need a real budget for technology over the next three years. That's _how much_. The money goes to student devices and the parent portal, not the front office. That's _where_. They need laptops and an online grade system. That's _what_. And the laptops come in year one, because the portal is useless if students don't have devices. That's _when_.

Here's the payoff. Once those four questions are answered, every technology dollar can be traced back to a goal. If a parent at a board meeting asks, 'Why did we spend this money?', the district can point to the Vision and give a clear answer. That's better alignment, and it's spending that actually makes sense."

## **Slide 17 - How Visions Compare**
"Now let's compare Visions to the other artifacts in the CSVLOD model, so we can see where they fit. Visions sit right next to three other types, and it's easy to mix them up, so it helps to see them side by side.

First, **Considerations**. Considerations describe _how_ an organization wants to work: its values, principles, and rules of the road. Visions go a step further. They describe _what_ the organization actually wants to do, and they come with a real plan.

Second, **Landscapes**. Landscapes are technical maps of the IT systems as they exist today. They're useful for IT, but a business leader wouldn't get much out of them. Visions look forward, not at the present, and they're written so business leaders can use them to steer technology.

Third, **Outlines**. An Outline zooms in on one specific project and describes it in detail. Visions stay zoomed out. They set the direction for the whole organization without getting into the details of any single project.

Let's run all three through our school district. A **Consideration** would be a principle like 'every student deserves equal access to technology.' A **Landscape** would be the IT team's diagram of the district's current network, servers, and software. An **Outline** would be the detailed plan for the laptop rollout: which schools go first, the budget, the timeline. And the **Vision** sits in the middle of all that. It's the big picture that says, 'Within three years, every student has a device and parents can check grades online.'

So you can think of the Vision as the one that connects the organization's values to the actual projects that get built."

## **Slide 18 - Five subtypes of Visions**
"So far we've talked about Visions in general. In practice, organizations use a few specific kinds, and the book names five of them. I'll give you a quick preview of each here, and the next few slides go into them in more detail.

First, a quick note on these colored tags. The book labels each type as **essential**, **common**, or **uncommon**, based on how often it shows up in organizations that do enterprise architecture well. Essential means you'll find it in almost every successful practice. Common means you'll see it often, but not everywhere. Uncommon means it's used, but more rarely. So the labels don't say whether a type is good or bad. They tell you how often it's used.

Here's the quick tour:

- **Business Capability Models** are a map of everything the organization can do, color-coded to show _where_ to invest.
- **Roadmaps** are a timeline of planned projects, showing _when_ to invest.
- **Target States** are a picture of where the organization wants to end up.
- **Value Chains** lay out the organization's activities in the order they add value, from start to finish.
- **Context Diagrams** are a one-page picture of how the business runs _today_.

Notice that the two essential ones work as a pair. One tells you _where_ to put your money, and the other tells you _when_. That's why nearly every organization uses both.

Let's start with the first one, Business Capability Models."


## **Slide 19 - Business Capability Models**
"Let's start with the first essential type: the Business Capability Model.

A Business Capability Model is a high-level view of everything an organization _can do_. It doesn't show who does it, what software they use, or who reports to whom. It just lists the organization's abilities.

Those abilities are arranged as a hierarchy, like the diagram here. At the top is the whole organization. Under it are big capabilities like Sales and Marketing, Customer Service, Operations, Finance, and Human Resources. Each of those can be broken down further. Customer Service, for example, might split into handling complaints, processing returns, and supporting customers online.

One of the most useful things about a capability model is that it's **stable**. Leaders come and go, departments get reorganized, and names on the org chart change, but what the organization needs to _do_ stays mostly the same. A company will always need to pay its employees, whoever runs HR.

The real power comes from **heatmapping**. Leaders and architects color-code the capabilities to show which ones need improvement. Red might mean 'this is struggling and needs investment,' and green might mean 'this is working fine, leave it alone.' Now the IT budget has a clear target.

Back to our school district. Its capability model might include Teaching and Learning, Student Services, Transportation, Food Services, and Finance. If the board heatmaps Teaching and Learning in red because students don't have devices, that tells everyone where the technology money should go first. And if a new superintendent arrives next year, the model still holds. The district still has to teach students and run buses.

That's also why these models create a **common language**. When a board member and an IT director both say 'Teaching and Learning,' they're pointing at the same box on the same page."


## **Slide 20 - Roadmaps**
"If the capability model tells us _where_ to invest, the Roadmap tells us _when_. It's the second essential type, and the two are almost always used together.

A Roadmap is a timeline of the IT projects an organization plans to take on, usually two to three years out. It's how a big strategic goal becomes a schedule of real projects that people can see and plan around.

Every project on a Roadmap moves through four stages, and the diagram here shows them in order.

- **Planned** means the idea has been placed on the Roadmap, but no real work has started yet.
- **Approved** means people have started exploring it seriously and sketching out how it might work.
- **Funded** means leadership has signed off and the money is set aside.
- **Active** means a team is building it right now.

Roadmaps are often color-coded by these stages, so anyone can glance at one and see which projects are just ideas and which ones are really happening.

Here's what that might look like for our school district. The laptop rollout is **Active**, and students are getting devices this year. The parent grade portal is **Funded**, with money approved to start next year. And a new online library system might just be **Planned**, an idea on the Roadmap for year three.

The last thing to know is that Roadmaps aren't set in stone. If the budget gets cut or a new priority comes up, projects can be moved, added, or dropped. That flexibility is a big part of why they work so well. The plan can change as fast as the organization does."

## **Slide 21 - Target States**
"The next type is the Target State, which the book labels _common_. If the Roadmap is the route, the Target State is the destination. It's a picture of where the organization wants to end up, usually about three years from now, from both the business side and the IT side.

A Target State doesn't spell out every technical detail. It paints the big picture: 'here's how things work today, and here's how we want them to work.' That picture then guides which projects go on the Roadmap.

That gives us a natural order for planning. First, the **capability model** shows _what_ needs to improve. Next, the **Target State** shows roughly _what it will look like_ once it's improved. Finally, the **Roadmap** lays out the _steps and timing_ to get there.

The book describes two kinds of Target States, shown at the bottom of the slide.

An **evolutionary** Target State is gradual improvement. The organization keeps working the same basic way, but upgrades the pieces, like replacing an old system with a better one.

A **transformational** Target State is a bigger shift that changes _how_ the organization is structured.

For our school district, an evolutionary Target State might be replacing an outdated gradebook with a modern one. Teachers and parents still use it the same way; it just works better. A transformational Target State would be every school in the district moving from its own separate systems onto one shared, district-wide platform. That changes how the whole district operates, not just the tools inside it.

The book also points out that Target States usually show up in more experienced organizations, because describing a clear future takes practice. That's part of why they're _common_ rather than _essential_."

## **Slide 22 - Value Chains & Context Diagrams**
"The last two types are both labeled _uncommon_. Organizations use them less often, but they're still useful, and each one gives a different way of looking at the business.

A **Value Chain** shows the organization's main activities in the order they happen, and how each step adds value. In the example here, materials come in from a supplier, go through operations, get distributed, and reach the customer.

In a lot of ways, a Value Chain does the same job as a capability model. It can even be color-coded to show which steps need investment. The difference is how it looks at the business. A capability model is a snapshot of what the organization _can_ do. A Value Chain shows the work _flowing_, step by step. One real advantage is that most business leaders already know the idea, since value chains are commonly taught in business schools. That makes them easy to introduce.

A **Context Diagram** is a one-page picture of how the organization operates _today_: who it works with and how everything connects. Here, the organization sits in the middle, surrounded by its customers, suppliers, partners, and employees.

The Context Diagram is the only Vision that describes the present instead of the future. It's still a Vision, though, because its job is to start a conversation about what should change. When everyone can see the whole picture on one page, problems and bottlenecks become much easier to spot.

For our school district, a Value Chain might follow a student from **enrollment**, to **instruction**, to **assessment**, to **graduation**. If assessment is slow and still done on paper, that's where IT could help. A Context Diagram would put the district in the middle, surrounded by students, parents, teachers, the state education department, and vendors. If parents are the group the district struggles to reach, that's exactly the kind of gap the parent grade portal is meant to fix."

## **Slide 23 - Getting it Right: Stay Abstract**
"Every artifact type in this book comes with a warning, and this is the one for Visions: the biggest mistake is making them **too detailed**.

That might sound backwards, since you'd think more detail means a better plan. But Visions look three to five years ahead, and nobody can predict that far out with much accuracy. Markets shift, budgets change, and new technology shows up. The book calls this the **EA uncertainty principle**: the further into the future you plan, the less detail you can realistically commit to. Any time spent planning the distant future in fine detail is usually wasted, because the plan goes out of date before anyone uses it.

So the book gives a simple rule of thumb. A Vision should have just enough detail to decide what gets funded in the **next budget cycle**, and no more. The detailed planning happens later, closer to when a project actually starts, in the Outlines and Designs.

That's also why the two essential types work so well. A capability model can be re-colored in an afternoon, and projects on a Roadmap can be moved around easily. They're built to change.

There's a real example of why this matters that every school remembers. Before 2020, a lot of districts had multi-year technology plans that rolled out devices slowly, a few grades at a time. Then the pandemic hit, and almost overnight every student needed a device and internet access at home. The districts with detailed, rigid plans had to tear them up. The ones whose Visions simply said 'every student should have access to technology for learning' still had a goal that made sense. They just sped up the timeline.

You might notice this mirrors the warning from Chapter 10. Standards fail when they get too rigid, and Visions fail when they get too detailed. In both cases, the goal is _just enough_."

## **Slide 24 - Chapter 11 Key Takeaways**
"Let's wrap up Chapter 11.

If you remember one sentence from this chapter, make it this one: **Visions connect business strategy with the future direction of IT.** Business leaders decide where the organization is going, IT decides how to get there, and the Vision is the shared picture that keeps both of them pointed the same way.

Each of the five types answers one simple question:

- A **Business Capability Model** asks, _what can we do_, and which of those things need investment?
- A **Roadmap** asks, _when do we do it?_
- A **Target State** asks, _where are we going?_
- A **Value Chain** asks, _how do we create value?_
- A **Context Diagram** asks, _how do we operate today?_

Think back to the problem we started with. Business and IT were working from different plans, and money went to projects that didn't match what the organization cared about. Visions solve that by putting everyone on the same page, literally, since most of them fit on one.

Our school district shows the whole idea from start to finish. The strategy was to give every student better access to learning. The Vision turned that into something concrete: devices for every student and a portal for parents. And that shaped the IT direction: which projects got funded, and in what order.

## **Slide 25 - Standards + Visions: Working Together**
"Before we finish, let's put both chapters side by side, because they're really two halves of the same job.

**Standards**, from Chapter 10, are about _how_ IT operates. They're the technical rules that keep things consistent: which technologies to use, how to use them, and how systems should be built. They guide the decisions made while a project is being built.

**Visions**, from Chapter 11, are about _where_ the organization is going. They connect business strategy to IT and guide which projects get funded and when.

Neither one works alone. Visions without Standards is a clear destination with no rules for the road, so every project gets built differently and the IT landscape turns into a mess. Standards without Visions is a very consistent IT department building the wrong things.

Let's run it through our school district one last time. The **Vision** says every student gets a laptop. The **Standards** make sure that happens the right way: every laptop is an approved model, every one is set up with the same security settings, and every one connects to the network the same way. The Vision decided _what_ to do, and the Standards made sure it was done _well_.

That's what enterprise architecture is all about: the business and IT agreeing on where to go, then getting there in a consistent, reliable way. And the lesson from both chapters is the same. Standards can't be too rigid, Visions can't be too detailed, and good architecture is always 'just enough.'"
