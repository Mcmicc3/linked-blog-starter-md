**EA Artifacts Related to Standards**

In enterprise architecture, Standards are IT-focused rules that describe how all technology in an organization should be built and used (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10). Organizations rarely write them from scratch. Instead, they adapt established industry standards to their own needs. Three common artifacts related to Standards are Technology Reference Models, Guidelines, and Patterns (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10).

A Technology Reference Model (TRM) is a one-page diagram of every technology an organization uses, grouped by function such as databases, servers, and security. Each technology is color-coded by status, such as current, retiring, or prohibited, so architects know what can be reused in new projects (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10). A real-world example is the Technical Reference Model in the U.S. Federal Enterprise Architecture (Office of Management and Budget, 2007). Another is the Thoughtworks (n.d.) Technology Radar, which sorts technologies into Adopt, Trial, Assess, and Hold, much like a TRM's status colors.

Guidelines are short, specific rules for how to use a particular technology. A TRM says what to use; a Guideline says how to use it (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10). Real-world sources include the OWASP Secure Coding Practices (OWASP Foundation, 2010), CIS Benchmarks for server hardening (Center for Internet Security, n.d.), NIST SP 800-63B-4 for authentication (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2025), and PCI DSS for protecting cardholder data (PCI Security Standards Council, 2024).

Patterns are proven, reusable solutions to recurring design problems. Each one explains the problem, when to apply it, why it works, and what the solution looks like (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10). Real-world examples include the _Enterprise Integration Patterns_ catalog (Hohpe & Woolf, 2003) and the cloud design patterns published by Microsoft (n.d.) and Amazon Web Services (n.d.).

Together, these artifacts create consistency, reduce costs and risks, and support compliance. However, overly strict Standards can slow projects down, so they must be applied with balance (Kotusev, 2021, Chapter 10).
