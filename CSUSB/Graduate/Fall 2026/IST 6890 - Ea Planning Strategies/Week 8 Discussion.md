**The Importance of Designs in Implementing IT Projects**

Designs are the essential bridge between architectural planning and the actual delivery of IT projects. Kotusev (2021) describes Designs as IT-focused artifacts that give detailed technical and functional descriptions of individual IT projects. Architects, project teams, and business representatives develop them together. Their importance lies in translating both business and technical requirements into actionable, implementation-ready plans.

From a business perspective, Designs capture and validate the specific functional and non-functional requirements a new system must meet. Business representatives, often working through business analysts, make sure these requirements are correct, consistent, and complete. They use techniques such as user stories, traceability matrices, and MoSCoW prioritization (Kotusev, 2021). This creates clear traceability between what the business requested and the capabilities that are ultimately delivered.

From a technical perspective, Designs ensure that projects comply with organizational Standards and integrate properly into existing Landscapes. They also keep projects aligned with higher-level artifacts such as Visions and Outlines (Kotusev, 2021). Architects focus on architectural fitness while project teams focus on practical feasibility, so a Design is a negotiated compromise between the ideal and the achievable. Kotusev argues that Designs are the only mechanism in an enterprise architecture practice that turns intangible architectural decisions into tangible IT systems. Without them, other architectural artifacts tend to be ignored, and the effort invested in them is wasted.

Designs also improve the quality of project delivery. They help identify risks early, serve as a single source of truth for all project participants, and make budgets and timelines more predictable (Kotusev, 2021). When projects are outsourced, Designs act as formal agreements against which contractors' work can be evaluated and accepted.

However, Kotusev (2021) cautions that Designs should stay architectural rather than excessively detailed. Overly detailed Designs can cause analysis paralysis, rigidity, and bureaucracy. At minimum, a Design should specify the technologies used, the system's interactions with the external IT environment, and its security considerations.

In summary, Designs are critical because they align business needs with technical decisions and reduce project risk. Above all, they ensure that enterprise architecture produces real, working systems.
